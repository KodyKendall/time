Moral. The work outlives the worker, and that is not a consolation — it is the whole point.

Devotion that nobody witnesses is not smaller for being unwitnessed. Her wife's ashes are in the north bank; the lawn she cut for forty years is the reason two strangers are lying in the grass at the end. She made the thing she'll never have, and she'll never know she made it.

How this departs from Tether

Tether had nine beats and a shape: bond, want, tension, snap, false victory, fall, all-is-lost, reconnection, climax. This film has no false victory and no fall. The tension comes from narrowing, not from an event. Nothing breaks. The lawn simply stops being hers.

  • Eleven beats, not nine — two of them are held images rather than action.
  • Two mirrored pairs carry the film instead of a plot: 1↔9 and 3↔11.
  • The sound carries the turn. The birds stop in beat 6 and never return.

Who she is

Locked to one generated character description and reused as a reference, as with Wren. She appears small or distant in 7 of 11 beats, which is the insurance policy: the film is mostly about the grass, and the grass is easy to keep consistent.

Never named. Neither is her wife. The only words in the film are the card, and they are spoken by no one.

No dialogue, no backstory beyond the bank. The film never says why she started.

The arc — two lines, not one

Her hours fall to zero and stay there. The grass only starts doing its work after that — the red line's entire climb happens in the last 26 seconds, and she is not in any of it. That gap is the entire moral, drawn.

Sound, which is doing the real work

  • Beats 1–5: birds. Dawn chorus, then a full Sunday park — dogs, greetings, a gate.
  • Beat 6, the turn: the birds stop mid-shot. The mower idles. Nothing under it.
  • Beats 7–9: birds never come back. Construction under silence.
  • Beat 10: total silence for 8 seconds. Then wind.
  • Beat 11: city. Traffic, distant voices, glass. No birds, no mower, no score.
  • No music at all until the card, and none after it.

The score — listen before anything is animated. Original, composed in code against this shot list at 60 BPM so one beat is one second and every cue below is the film's own timecode.

THE STRIPE is the entire score: two notes a tone apart — F then G — six-tenths apart, repeating every 1.2 seconds. It is the sound of a mower making a pass and turning. It is also the sound of a stripe: an alternation, forever, going nowhere in particular. The mower sound effect runs on that same 1.2-second cycle, so the machine and the music are one rhythm.

Open questions for the director

1. Does she ever smile?

The single biggest undecided thing in the film. Right now she is content but unsmiling in Act one, which reads as dignity. If she smiles in beat 5 when the dog comes over, the whole film warms by about ten degrees and the ending hits harder. If she never smiles at all, she becomes a martyr and the moral curdles. My recommendation: one smile, in beat 5, only.

2. The word "we".

See beat 10. We / I / they. Ambiguity is good here but it should be a decision, not an oversight.

3. Title.

Working title is The Long Grass. Alternatives: North Bank (colder, more formal), Forty Years of Stripes (states the theme outright — probably too literal), The Last Stripe (centres the ending, spoils it slightly).

4. Beat 5, the crowd.

It is the best argument in the film and the plate most likely to look bad. Options: keep it full, thin it to two figures and one dog, or replace it with something static — a bench with a person's things still on it, which would also give her a reason to keep coming. My recommendation: keep it, but generate it three ways and pick.

5. Runtime.

2:58 as written. Beat 11 could go to 45 seconds and the film would improve; beat 3 could lose 4 seconds and nobody would notice.
