# Stephen King Short Story Method

Paraphrased working notes from Stephen King's *On Writing: A Memoir of the Craft* (2000), "Everything You Need to Know About Writing Successfully — in Ten Minutes" (*The Writer*, 1986), and related interviews / story introductions.

Use this skill to guide short-story writing: idea → fast discovery draft → rest → revision → polish. Do not paste long copyrighted passages. Paraphrase, use short quotes only.

## Core ideas

1. **Read a lot, write a lot.** King prescribes ~4-6 hours reading and ~2,000 words / ~6-10 pages writing daily, every day including holidays. For short stories, scale the quota down (e.g. 500-1,000 words/day) but keep the daily streak.
2. **Stories are found, not built.** "Stories are found things, like fossils in the ground ... Stories are relics, part of an undiscovered pre-existing world." Seashell or T-Rex — short story or thousand-page novel — "the techniques of excavation remain basically the same."
3. **Distrust plot.** Plot is "the good writer's last resort and the dullard's first choice," a "jackhammer" that breaks as much as it frees. Prefer intuition and discovery.
4. **Situation first, characters second, theme last.** Start with a `What if...?` situation that cannot stay static. Add flat, unfeatured characters. Watch what they do, write it down. Theme and meaning emerge — reinforce them only in revision.
5. **Best stories are about people, not the event.** Put contradictory, motivated people inside pressure and let character generate plot.
6. **The toolbox:** vocabulary (use the first right word, never hunt a thesaurus mid-draft), grammar (plain over fancy — "Language does not always have to wear a tie and lace-up shoes"), avoid passive voice, and cut adverbs — especially after dialogue tags. "To write adverbs is human, to write 'he said' or 'she said' is divine."
7. **Three parts:** Narration moves the story A→B. Description creates sensory reality. Dialogue brings characters to life. Paragraph — not sentence — is the unit of coherence.
8. **Description begins in the writer's imagination but should end in the reader's.** Give enough to see, leave room to dream. Don't over-explain looks, backstory, or theme.
9. **Write to entertain.** Serious ideas must serve the story, never the reverse. "If you want to preach, get a soapbox."
10. **Write the hard truth.** "The most important things are the hardest things to say." Dig where you feel ashamed or evasive — that is usually the story.

## The workflow

### 1. Find a situation (15-30 min)

Brainstorm `What if...?` seeds from ordinary life tipped sideways:

- What if vampires invaded a small New England village? (*'Salem's Lot*)
- What if a mother and son were trapped in a stalled car by a rabid dog? (*Cujo*)
- What if a dome dropped over a small town?

Rules:
- One sentence. Person + pressure + place.
- No outline, no ending required. Know the starting situation only.
- Pick the one you can't stop thinking about. Ask: "Am I having fun?" If always no, pick another project.

Short-story test: the situation must be playable in 1,000-7,500 words. One pressure, 1-3 characters, one arc. If it needs three towns and ten years, save it for a novel.

### 2. First draft — door closed

Write for yourself, fast, private. No audience in your head.

- Same time, same place daily. Desk in the corner, door shut, distractions out (no phone/TV). Music if it walls the world out.
- No reference books, dictionary, thesaurus, or research mid-draft. Misspell, use a placeholder city, keep moving: `Miami [CHECK LATER]`.
- See the story scene by scene, not word by word. Narrate paragraph by paragraph.
- Dialogue: let each character talk differently; how they say it is who they are.
- Finish in as few sittings as possible — ideally one sitting for a short story, max a few days. Speed keeps enthusiasm high and doubt out.
- Do not show anyone. Do not discuss plot to "drain" it. Protect narrative energy.
- Ideal Reader (King's is Tabitha King) stays out of the room for now — write what *you* think they'd feel, but don't ask yet.

Output: a complete messy draft. It will be puffy. That is expected.

### 3. Cool off — drawer time

King prescribes 6 weeks for a novel so you return as a reader, not the author. For a short story, scale but don't skip:

- Minimum: overnight to 1 week. Better: 1-2 weeks working on something else.
- Do not re-read during the rest.
- Take a real break: have fun, relax, read others.

Goal feeling: "oddly familiar yet unrecognizable, like the work of someone else who writes suspiciously like you."

### 4. Second draft — door open

Read the whole draft in one sitting if possible.

Ask big questions first:
- Is this coherent?
- What is it actually about? What will turn it into a song?
- What are the recurring elements? Do they entwine into a theme?
- Where is the resonance — what lingers after the last line?
- Character motivation: would this person really do this? Mark violations (`Sandy would never do this!`) for full fixes.

Then fix small things:
- Grammar, spelling, factual errors, plot holes.
- Cut adverbs, passive constructions, throat-clearing openings.
- Cut detailed character-description dumps.
- Strengthen theme only after naming it: add / sharpen 1-2 scenes or planted details that pay off later.

### 5. The 10% rule

Apply King's editor's formula, learned early from a rejection slip ("Not bad, but PUFFY. You need to revise for length"):

```text
2nd Draft = 1st Draft – 10%
```

John Gould lesson: King went from 2,500-word first drafts swelling to 3,300 words, to 2,500 → 2,200. "I only took out the bad parts."

Practical cut pass:
1. Count words. Target = ~90%.
2. Cut whole scenes / paragraphs that repeat known info first.
3. Then sentences, then adverbs, adjectives, "very / really / suddenly / forcefully."
4. If you remove excess and can't find the point, tear it up and restart — "If it's bad, kill it."

### 6. Feedback — the Ideal Reader test

- Give the second draft to ~5-10 trusted readers. Demand they wait until you're ready to hear it.
- Tally: if most flag the *same* problem (false twist, stilted arc, dead character) — fix it, even if you love it. If everyone flags *different* things — you can safely disregard most of it.
- Incorporate selectively, polish. King's standard: two drafts + final polish; repeat cycles as needed.

### 7. Submit like a pro (King's market rules, modernized)

- Know the markets: don't send vampire bats to a literary romance mag. Read the magazine before submitting.
- Manuscript neat, correctly formatted, spell-checked. Follow each market's guidelines exactly (modern version of "return postage, SASE").
- Flog stories around yourself early; no agent needed until there is money to steal.
- Track rejections. Personal notes / callbacks = getting warmer. After ~hundreds of form rejects with no warmth, re-evaluate the story or move on — don't self-delude, but don't quit at 6 or 60.

## Short-story checklist

Before calling done, verify:

- [ ] Starts from a single What-if situation, not a moral or twist outline
- [ ] 1-3 characters, each with distinct voice
- [ ] Narration + description + dialogue all working (moves, senses, lives)
- [ ] No thesaurus words, few adverbs, "said" left alone
- [ ] Drafted fast with door closed, rested, revised with door open
- [ ] ~10% cut from first draft
- [ ] Theme named and reinforced, not preached
- [ ] Ends with resonance — image / line that lingers
- [ ] Fun test passed at least sometimes; hard truth faced
- [ ] Read aloud once for rhythm and false dialogue

## What NOT to do

- Don't outline the twist beat-by-beat and force characters into it.
- Don't revise while drafting or show chapter-by-chapter for approval.
- Don't stop mid-draft to research or synonym-hunt.
- Don't keep a beloved scene everyone trips over.
- Don't explain the theme in narration.
- Don't confuse passing tests / word count with quality — resonance is the metric.

## Example prompts for the assistant

- "Give me 5 King-style What-if situations about [suburb / marriage / small town]."
- "Draft a 1,500-word discovery draft from this situation: [X]. Door closed — no stopping, no polish."
- "I've rested this draft [paste]. Do an open-door read: coherence, theme, recurring elements, motivation flags, and a 10% cut list."
- "Cut 10% from this draft while preserving voice. List what you removed and why."
